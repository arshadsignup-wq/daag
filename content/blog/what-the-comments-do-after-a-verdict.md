A verdict gets published. Within a few hours, underneath it, a second thing happens that is not reporting and is not justice. It is a comment section, and it follows a shape regular enough to predict.

This archive exists because that second thing is a public act by a named person, and because it disappears when someone decides it should.

## The shape it takes

Across the entries in [this archive](/archive), the same handful of moves come up again and again under reporting about gender violence:

- **Relocating the question to the woman.** What was she doing out, what was she wearing, why was she there at that hour. The subject quietly changes from what the accused did to what she should not have done.
- **Attacking the delay.** Why did she wait, why now, why did she not go to the police the same night. The three-and-a-half-year average a case actually takes is never applied to the question of why anyone hesitates.
- **Disputing the record itself.** The screenshots are edited, the story is arranged, the press is selling something. This one survives even a conviction.
- **Generalising from one case.** A single false-case verdict becomes proof about every complaint ever filed.
- **Threats and address-hunting.** Less frequent, far more serious, and the point at which a comment stops being speech about a story and becomes a plan.

None of those are rare. They are the ordinary weather of a Bangladeshi comment section after this kind of story.

## What the data says about who gets hit

Of everyone recorded as a victim of cyber violence in Bangladesh in the first six months of 2025, **97 percent were women and children**. The figure comes from a platform convened by the Bangladesh Legal Aid and Services Trust with fourteen organisations including BRAC, Naripokkho, Bangladesh Mahila Parishad and Ain o Salish Kendra, analysing 29 documented incidents ([Prothom Alo, 9 July 2025](https://www.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/7gdiyso4ho)).

Separately, ActionAid found that about **64 percent** of women online had faced violence there, up fourteen percentage points on the year before, and that **85 percent of them never complained to anyone** ([Prothom Alo, 27 November 2022](https://www.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/p5uqt9qjl3)).

That last number is the one that matters most for anyone deciding whether documentation is worth the trouble. The overwhelming majority of this is never reported to anyone at all, which means the official record of online abuse in Bangladesh is a small sample of a much larger thing.

The Daily Star has called online abuse a national crisis and argued the legal framework has not kept up ([8 March 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/supplements/international-womens-day-2026/news/online-abuse-now-national-crisis-time-act-4122986)), and documented the specific gaps in law and enforcement that let cyber violence against women continue ([17 December 2025](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/bangladesh/news/gaps-laws-enforcement-fuel-cyber-violence-against-women-4061176)).

## The one that needs care

In October a Netrokona tribunal convicted a woman of filing a false rape case, in a matter dating back to 2008 ([The Daily Star, 13 October 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/crime-justice/news/netrokona-woman-jailed-filing-false-rape-case-4297881)).

That is a real judgment and it should be reported as one. Within hours it also becomes the single most reused argument in comment sections: proof, supposedly, that complaints in general cannot be trusted.

It is worth being precise about what it does and does not establish, because the imprecision is the whole point of the reuse:

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| A court found **this** complaint false | What the judgment says |
| Acquittal means the complaint was false | **No.** Different legal conclusions, different standards of proof |
| A 70 percent acquittal rate means 70 percent lied | **No.** No court has found that, and the research attributes acquittals largely to delay and evidentiary failure |

Conviction for a false case is a specific finding, reached after a separate trial. Treating it as a statement about complainants generally is not a reading of the judgment. It is a use of it.

Daag records that use when it happens publicly under someone's real name. Not because the opinion is forbidden, but because it is a public act with an author, and the authorship is what tends to vanish.

## How to document a comment properly

If you are going to record something, record it so it holds up. The archive's own standard, and the reason entries get rejected:

1. **Capture the comment before anything else.** Full screenshot, showing the comment, the account name, and enough of the surrounding thread that context is visible. Not a crop of the words alone.
2. **Copy the link to the source post**, not just the comment. The post is what lets a moderator open it and check.
3. **Copy the link to the commenter's profile.** A display name is not an identifier; two people share a name, a profile does not.
4. **Do not edit the image.** Not to highlight, not to blur, not to crop out the timestamp. An edited screenshot cannot be verified and will be rejected.
5. **Type the comment out if you can.** The screenshot is the proof; the transcription is what makes it findable in a search later. It is optional, and an entry without it is still valid.
6. **Record it while it is public.** A comment that has been deleted cannot be verified against its source, and an entry that cannot be verified does not get published.

The requirement that trips most submissions is the second one. A screenshot with no source link is a picture of text, and a picture of text is not evidence of who wrote it.

## What happens after you submit

Nothing is published automatically. A submission goes into a queue, a moderator opens the links, checks whether the screenshot has been altered, settles the category, and then approves, rejects, or asks for more. Typical turnaround is three to five working days, and you get a reference number to check where yours has got to.

Your email and your relationship to the incident are never shown publicly, and the person named in an entry cannot see who reported them. If they believe an entry is wrong, they can [appeal](/appeals), and a moderator other than the one who approved it reviews it within fourteen working days.

The full set of questions people ask before submitting is answered on the [about page](/about).

## Why bother

Because the comment outlives the moment it was written in, and because the person who wrote it usually does not expect it to.

A conviction takes [three and a half years on average](/blog/180-days-in-law-three-years-in-practice). A comment takes nine seconds. The asymmetry between those two numbers is most of what this archive is about: the public reaction to gender violence moves at a speed the justice system cannot match, shapes what survivors expect before they ever reach a police station, and leaves almost no durable record of itself.

Daag is a record, not a verdict and not a punishment. It takes no position on guilt in any criminal matter, publishes no private data, and asks that entries not be used to harass anyone.
