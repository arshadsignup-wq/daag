Around midday on a Monday in October, a woman was assaulted on the footpath opposite Bangladesh Eye Hospital on Satmasjid Road in Dhanmondi. An assistant sub-inspector was present at the scene. He was later withdrawn from his post over allegations of negligence of duty ([Prothom Alo, 14 October 2026](https://en.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/crime-and-law/5ks378n363)).

Video of the incident circulated widely. What followed is the part worth setting out carefully, because it was widely described online in a way the reporting does not support.

## What actually happened, in order

The sequence matters, so here it is as reported:

1. A woman was harassed in public, in daylight, with a police officer present who did not intervene.
2. She contacted a relative.
3. The relative confronted the man accused of harassing her and assaulted him.
4. **The relative was detained and sentenced to 15 days in jail.**
5. The officer who had been present was withdrawn from his post for negligence.
6. Separately, a young man was detained over the footpath assault and produced before a metropolitan magistrate.

Police also said the man accused of the harassment suffers from a mental illness.

The woman's own description of where this left her, in a written statement she sent to Prothom Alo and also posted on social media, was a question: "Then are we the ones who have committed a crime by trying to protect myself?" ([Prothom Alo, 15 October 2026](https://en.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/city/solae1k2ja)).

## Being accurate about it

The version that travelled fastest online was that a woman was jailed for defending herself. That is not what happened, and repeating it does the case no favours.

The person jailed was a relative, and what he was jailed for was assaulting someone. Those are real facts and they matter, both to him and to the man he assaulted, who police say is mentally ill.

What is also true, and is the thing the woman's question actually points at, is the shape of the outcome: the harassment produced no immediate consequence for anyone, the officer who watched it produced a transfer, and the only custodial sentence in the first days went to the person who intervened.

You do not have to believe the relative should have escaped consequences to notice that ordering. A system can be applying the law correctly in each individual step and still produce a sequence that teaches women not to expect protection.

## The vicarious lesson

This is the mechanism that makes incidents like this one matter beyond the people involved.

Most women will never be assaulted on Satmasjid Road. Nearly all of them will see the video and read the comments, and will take from it a conclusion about what happens if they ask for help in public. That conclusion is formed long before anyone reaches a police station, and it is formed by the reaction as much as by the event.

Prothom Alo has reported on women facing security risks amid both mob violence and online attacks ([8 January 2026](https://en.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/io0fojls6x)), and The Daily Star has called online abuse a national crisis ([8 March 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/supplements/international-womens-day-2026/news/online-abuse-now-national-crisis-time-act-4122986)).

Of everyone recorded as a victim of cyber violence in Bangladesh in the first half of 2025, **97 percent were women and children**, according to a platform convened by the Bangladesh Legal Aid and Services Trust with fourteen organisations ([Prothom Alo, 9 July 2025](https://www.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/7gdiyso4ho)).

## What the comments did

A video like this produces a predictable split, and both halves are documented in [this archive](/archive).

- **What was she doing there.** Applied to a public footpath at midday, which is roughly the safest set of circumstances available, and still applied.
- **It is staged.** The claim that the video is arranged, that the woman is an actor, or that the whole thing is a campaign. This survives the presence of a police officer and a magistrate.
- **She should be grateful.** Directed at a woman who said she did not feel protected.
- **Sympathy that turns into a demand.** Support for the woman, followed quickly by instructions about how she should now behave, dress, or travel.

Daag does not record these because the opinions are forbidden. It records them because they are public statements by named accounts, they shape what the next woman expects, and they are routinely deleted once a story stops trending.

## If you see something like this

The practical version, for anyone who wants the record to survive:

- **Capture the comment, not just the video.** The video will be reposted by many accounts. The comment, with its author, usually exists in one place and gets removed.
- **Include the source post link.** A screenshot with no link cannot be verified, and an entry that cannot be verified does not get published here.
- **Do not edit the image.** No cropping out the timestamp, no highlighting. An altered screenshot is not evidence.
- **Act while it is public.** Deleted comments cannot be checked against their source.
