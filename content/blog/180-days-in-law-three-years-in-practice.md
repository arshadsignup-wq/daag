Bangladesh's Women and Children Repression Prevention Act, 2000 requires a tribunal to finish a case within 180 working days. Research presented at the BRAC Centre in Dhaka found that a case actually takes an average of **three years and seven months**, across an average of twenty-two hearing dates ([Prothom Alo, 2 May 2026](https://www.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/ebsz0axkin)).

That single gap explains most of what people mean when they say the system does not work. It is also why so many women are told, by the people closest to them, that there is no point starting.

October 2026 happened to produce a set of verdicts that show the spread unusually clearly.

## What the courts decided in October

Four judgments reported in the second half of the month, with the time from offence to verdict:

| Verdict | Reported | Offence dated | Elapsed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Three men jailed for life for a gang rape on a moving bus in Tangail, fined Tk 2 lakh each | [25 Oct 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/crime-justice/news/3-get-life-gang-raping-woman-moving-bus-tangail-4307206) | 14 January 2026 | about 9 months |
| An imam jailed for life in Jamalpur for raping a 15-year-old student | [26 Oct 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/crime-justice/news/imam-gets-life-term-over-rape-girl-4307536) | November and December 2020 | about 6 years |
| A man jailed for life in Gopalganj for raping a 15-year-old girl | [15 Oct 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/crime-justice/news/man-gets-life-sentence-raping-15-year-old-girl-gopalganj-4299456) | 16 December 2023 | about 3 years |

All three ended in conviction. All three were reported within eleven days of each other. And the distance between them, nine months against six years, is the thing worth paying attention to.

For comparison, in July a tribunal in Mymensingh delivered three death sentences **twenty-five days** after the incident ([Prothom Alo, 9 July 2026](https://www.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/district/io0nn0q3wi)).

Twenty-five days and six years are both real outcomes under the same statute.

## The 180 days is a direction, not a deadline

There is a legal reason the clock slips, and it is not widely discussed.

Section 20 of the Act says a tribunal shall complete a trial within 180 days of receiving the case. Section 31A says that if it does not, the tribunal must record the reasons and file a report with the Supreme Court within thirty days.

In September 2026 the High Court held that the 180-day provision is **directory, not mandatory**: a trial that runs past it is not void, and the delay does not by itself entitle an accused to have proceedings quashed. The ruling came in a petition filed back in 2011 by an accused seeking exactly that, on the grounds that the deadline had passed and no report had been filed ([Prothom Alo, 23 September 2026](https://www.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/7ilfivbbpy)).

The practical effect is worth stating plainly. The deadline is real, the reporting duty is real, and neither carries a consequence that stops a case from drifting. A tribunal that misses 180 days files a reason. A tribunal that files a reason carries on.

That is how a statute can promise six months and deliver three and a half years without anyone breaking it.

## Why the fast ones are fast

A case that finishes quickly is usually a case where three things happened to line up: the accused was arrested early, the medical and forensic evidence was collected while it still existed, and the witnesses stayed reachable and willing.

None of those are guaranteed by the law. They are guaranteed by circumstance.

The Mymensingh case moved in twenty-five days because the accused were in custody and the evidence was fresh. The Jamalpur case took six years because, as the prosecution described it, the offences dated to late 2020 and the case had to travel the full distance of an ordinary tribunal docket.

The law's 180-day clock does not start the engine. It only tells you how far behind schedule the engine already is.

## What happens to the rest

The same research puts the conviction rate in cases under the Act at **3 percent**, with roughly **70 percent** of defendants acquitted.

A 70 percent acquittal rate is not evidence that 70 percent of complaints were untrue. It is mostly evidence of what three and a half years does to a case: witnesses move, memories blur, physical evidence degrades or was never collected properly, and families under pressure settle or stop attending. Several of those are failures of investigation, not of the complaint.

The Daily Star followed one case through to its end: a twenty-year fight that closed in July 2025 with the accused acquitted for insufficient evidence ([25 September 2025](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/bangladesh/news/justice-eludes-women-children-trials-drag-3995001)).

Twenty years is not a trial. It is a sentence served by the complainant.

## The false-case verdict, and what it does not prove

On 13 October a Netrokona tribunal sentenced a woman to one year of rigorous imprisonment and a Tk 5,000 fine for filing a false rape case. The case she had filed dated to 2008 ([The Daily Star, 13 Oct 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/crime-justice/news/netrokona-woman-jailed-filing-false-rape-case-4297881); [Prothom Alo, 14 Oct 2026](https://www.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/district/vda9kiabbr)).

That verdict is a real finding by a court and it deserves to be reported accurately. It also gets used, within hours of publication, to argue something it does not support.

Two things are worth separating:

1. **A court found this particular complaint false.** That is what the judgment says.
2. **An acquittal is not a finding that a complaint was false.** Those are different legal conclusions, reached by different standards. Conflating them turns a 70 percent acquittal rate into a 70 percent lying rate, which is not what the number means and not what any court has found.

The distance between those two sentences is where most of the comment section lives.

## What this means if you are deciding whether to report

Nothing above is an argument against filing. It is an argument for knowing the shape of the thing you are starting.

- **The clock in the statute is not the clock you will experience.** Plan for years, not months, unless the accused is arrested immediately.
- **Evidence collected in the first days decides most of it.** Medical examination, the scene, the phone, the screenshots. What is not collected early is usually not recoverable later.
- **Keep your own copies of everything**, including anything posted publicly about the case. Posts get deleted. Screenshots do not.
- **The reference number matters.** Whatever you file, keep the number and the date. It is how you find out where the case has got to without depending on anyone's memory.
