In October a CNG-run auto-rickshaw driver gave a confessional statement to a magistrate in Cumilla, admitting to the rape and murder of a 15-year-old schoolgirl whose body was found in a paddy field in Lalmai ([The Daily Star, 11 October 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/crime-justice/news/cng-driver-confesses-rape-murder-schoolgirl-4295571)).

For most readers, that is where the story ends. A man confessed. It is solved.

On the same day, Prothom Alo published a piece titled "Many questions remain unanswered" ([11 October 2026](https://en.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/local-news/shxvqywpcb)).

Both are accurate. Understanding why is useful, and not only for this case.

*As with other cases involving children, this piece does not repeat the victim's name, though it has been widely published.*

## What is known

Police said the girl hired the driver's auto-rickshaw from Paduar Bazar to Racecourse for Tk 300 at around 12:30am. The driver was arrested at his brother's house in Chattogram, and police recovered the phone taken from the girl's bag from a location he identified ([The Daily Star, 10 October 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/crime-justice/news/driver-arrested-over-murder-cumilla-girl-4294736)).

The confession was given before a Senior Judicial Magistrate in Cumilla.

## What the confession does not answer

Prothom Alo's follow-up set out the gaps plainly. Two stand out:

- **The route.** The destination was about eight kilometres away. The body was found after what the reporting describes as a roughly thirty-kilometre detour, in the opposite direction from the city.
- **Whether anyone else was involved**, which the investigation had not resolved.

There is a further point in that report worth repeating: the girl's family had lived in Cumilla for years, so she would have had some familiarity with the local geography. A thirty-kilometre detour in the wrong direction is not obviously something a local passenger fails to notice.

None of this means the confession is false. It means a confession is a piece of evidence, not a conclusion.

## What a section 164 statement actually is

In Bangladeshi criminal procedure, a confessional statement recorded by a magistrate under section 164 of the Code of Criminal Procedure is a formal, recorded admission. The magistrate is required to satisfy himself that it is voluntary and to warn the person that it may be used against him.

Three things follow that are routinely missed in public discussion:

| | |
|---|---|
| A confession can be **retracted** | Retracted confessions are common, and a court must then decide what weight to give it |
| A confession usually needs **corroboration** | Courts are cautious about convicting on a retracted confession alone, without supporting evidence |
| A confession binds **the person who made it** | It is not, by itself, evidence against a co-accused |

That third point is why the open question about whether anyone else was involved matters. If the investigation closes on the confession, any other participant becomes materially harder to charge later.

## Why this gets flattened online

The gap between "a man confessed" and "the investigation has unanswered questions" is where a lot of public argument happens, in both directions.

One side treats the confession as the end: the case is closed, further questions are an insult to the victim, and anyone raising them is making excuses. The other treats the unanswered questions as proof the whole thing is staged.

Neither follows. An investigation can produce a real confession and still be incomplete. Saying so is not scepticism about the victim; it is the ordinary condition of a case that is still being investigated.

Daag records the public reaction to cases like this one, not the case itself. The reaction worth recording here is not the argument about the route. It is the reliable arrival, within hours of any report involving a girl out at night, of comments about what she was doing in an auto-rickshaw at half past midnight.

That question is directed at a dead child. It is also, in the pattern this archive documents, one of the three or four most common responses to this category of story.

## For anyone following a case

A few things make it easier to follow reporting on a criminal case without being misled:

1. **Separate the stages.** Arrest, confession, charge framing, trial, verdict, and appeal are different events. Reporting on one is routinely read as the conclusion of all of them.
2. **Note who is speaking.** "Police said" is not a finding of fact. A press briefing describes an allegation at that moment in an investigation.
3. **Watch for the retraction.** It is common and it is rarely reported with the same prominence as the confession.
4. **A death sentence is not final at the trial court.** It goes to the High Court Division for confirmation, as happened in [the Pallabi case](/blog/nineteen-days-what-the-fastest-trial-required).
5. **Screenshot what you want to keep.** Reporting is edited and comment threads are deleted. If you intend to cite something later, capture it now.
