On 19 May 2026 an eight-year-old girl was killed in Pallabi, Dhaka, after being raped. On 7 June, nineteen days later, a tribunal sentenced two people to death for it. The Law Minister called it the fastest trial in Bangladesh's history ([The Daily Star, 7 June 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/crime-justice/news/ramisa-murder-sohel-wife-swapna-sentenced-death-4192556)).

Set that against the average. Research presented at the BRAC Centre in Dhaka found that a case under the Women and Children Repression Prevention Act takes an average of **three years and seven months**, across twenty-two hearing dates ([Prothom Alo, 2 May 2026](https://www.prothomalo.com/bangladesh/ebsz0axkin)).

Nineteen days against three and a half years is not a small difference. It is worth understanding exactly what produced it, because the answer is less encouraging than the headline.

**A note on naming.** This case is universally reported under the child's name. Daag does not repeat the names of children who were raped or killed, including where the press has published them. Section 14 of the Act restricts identifying victims, and an archive built around how people are spoken about publicly should hold itself to that even when others do not. The case is referred to here by where it happened.

## What the record shows

| | |
|---|---|
| Incident | 19 May 2026, Pallabi, Dhaka |
| Verdict | 7 June 2026 |
| Elapsed | 19 days |
| Hearings | 5 |
| Prosecution witnesses | 16 |
| Sentence | Death for both accused, aged 31 and 26 |
| Fines | Tk 5 lakh and Tk 2 lakh, ordered paid to the victim's legal heirs |

The judge was Masrur Salekin of the Children Violence Suppression Tribunal in Dhaka Metropolitan, who wrapped up proceedings in five hearings ([The Daily Star, 5 June 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/crime-justice/news/ramisa-murder-verdict-sunday-after-19-days-4191026)). The sixteen witnesses included the child's parents and elder sister, three neighbours, a doctor, a magistrate and the investigating officer.

The detail worth pausing on is the fine. The tribunal ordered it paid to the victim's legal heirs, which is a form of redress the Act allows and which almost never gets reported.

## What made it fast

Four things happened here that do not usually happen together.

**The accused were in custody immediately.** Nothing in a tribunal can move while the accused is absconding. A warrant that cannot be executed is the single most common reason a case sits still for years.

**The evidence was fresh.** Medical and forensic evidence in a rape case degrades on a timescale of days. When the examination happens at once and the scene is preserved, the prosecution has something to put in front of a judge. When it happens weeks later, often it does not.

**The witnesses were available and willing.** Sixteen witnesses across five hearings means nobody had to be summoned repeatedly. In an ordinary case, the average of twenty-two hearing dates is largely a record of witnesses who did not come.

**The state wanted it.** There was national outrage, and the government publicly committed to a fast trial.

Only the first three are things the justice system controls. The fourth is the one that decided this case, and it is not available to most complainants. A case becomes fast when it becomes politically costly to be slow.

## Why the case is not over

The reporting mostly stopped at the verdict. Two days later something procedural but important happened: the death reference was sent to the Supreme Court ([The Daily Star, 9 June 2026](https://www.thedailystar.net/news/crime-justice/news/ramisa-rape-murder-death-reference-sent-supreme-court-4194481)).

A death reference is mandatory. A death sentence passed by a trial court has no effect until the High Court Division confirms it. The convicts may also appeal. So the nineteen-day figure describes the trial court stage only, and the final outcome is not yet known.

This matters for how the case gets discussed. A sentence that is pending confirmation is routinely described online as settled, final and carried out. It is none of those things yet.

## What the comments did with it

This is the part Daag records.

A case this fast, this public and this severe produces a very particular reaction underneath the reporting. Some of it is grief. A large share of it is something else: demands for extrajudicial punishment, arguments that trials are a waste of time, and, within a day, the familiar pivot to what the child's family should have done differently.

That last move is the one worth noticing, because an eight-year-old leaves almost no room for it. The reasoning does not stop when the victim is a child; it relocates to the mother, to the neighbourhood, to the family's circumstances.

[The archive](/archive) exists to keep that second record. Not the crime, which the courts and the press handle, but what named people wrote publicly underneath it, with a screenshot and a link to the source post, reviewed by a person before publication.

## The honest conclusion

Nineteen days proves the system can move. It does not prove the system does move.

Everything that made this case fast was circumstantial: an immediate arrest, intact evidence, willing witnesses, and sustained public attention. Strip out the public attention and you have an ordinary case, and [the ordinary case takes three and a half years](/blog/180-days-in-law-three-years-in-practice) if it survives at all.

The useful question after a verdict like this one is not whether justice is possible. It is why the conditions for it have to be exceptional.
